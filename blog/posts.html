ALL CODE IS NOW OPEN SOURCE

Not legally. Practically.

For a long time, we assumed that compiling code and hiding the source code protected it.

AI changes this.

AI can take a binary, decompile it, and reconstruct readable source code that behaves like the original.

All code you ship is now effectively open source.

Open source may have won without everyone agreeing to open-source their code.

September 9, 2026

Okay, allow me to brag a little today. Please excuse me. :-)

Today, Silq is the leading embedded-finance platform in Saudi Arabia.

And I may have been the first to conceive and patent embedded checkout, an early precursor to today’s embedded finance.

Here is that story.

In 2008, paying through a third-party payment service such as PayPal typically meant leaving the merchant's website. Customers clicked a payment button, were redirected to the payment provider's website, authenticated themselves, gave the payment info, and then returned to complete the purchase.

I was working on Amazon Pay at that time and thought there had to be a better way.

My idea was to embed checkout within the merchant's website instead of redirecting the customer elsewhere.

The payment provider should be able to present the checkout experience inside the merchant's page. Customers should be able to use their stored payment and shipping info, while their sensitive credentials travel directly to the payment provider and remain invisible to the merchant.

I came up with two ways of achieving this.

1. Quick Checkout (U.S. Patent No. 10528931)

The "Quick Checkout" architecture used provider-controlled components embedded within the merchant's page. Sensitive information went directly to the provider, rather than passing through the merchant.

The architectural principle eventually became standard across modern payments:

- Braintree Hosted Fields uses provider-controlled iFrames, so raw card information never reaches the merchant's server.
- Stripe Elements uses Stripe-hosted components embedded within the merchant's checkout, with sensitive card details going directly to Stripe.
- Adyen similarly embeds provider-managed payment experiences within merchants' websites.

2. Instant Checkout (U.S. Patent No. 9324098)

Instant Checkout took the idea further. The payment provider could recognize the customer through its cookie, retrieve the customer’s default payment and shipping information, and deliver a one-click experience within the merchant’s website.

That idea also grew into a major category:

- Bolt created a cross-merchant shopper network offering embedded one-click checkout.
- Shop Pay made stored payment and shipping info reusable across Shopify merchants.
- PayPal also allowed customers to complete purchases without leaving the merchant experience.

Embedded checkout brought payments into the purchasing journey. Embedded financing brings capital into the same journey.

Both reflect the same conviction: services should appear naturally at the moment of need - not force customers into a separate process.

August 10, 2026· Quoting @daniel_mac8

Instead of STE-100, use this for your AI-written documents.

The voice: simple yet powerful, not marketing-y. One clear mind explaining something plainly, persuading through reasoning rather than adjectives. A smart outsider should follow it instantly with no prior context.

The writing tenets:
- Short sentences. One idea per sentence; prefer several short ones over a long one.
- Full sentences, never fragments. No telegraphic "Term: clipped phrase" bullets.
- Plain words, no buzzwords. Cut leverage, agentic, AI-native, seamless, robust, and the rest. If a term is unavoidable, define it right there.
- Assume no prior knowledge. Explain every concept and name as you introduce it.
- Problem first. Today's state and why it is a problem, then what you build, then the benefit, in that order.
- Be concrete. Real numbers and a worked example over abstraction.
- Causal logic. Say "X, because Y." Explain the link; do not just assert it.
- No decoration, no throat-clearing. Cut hype adjectives and filler openers.
- Be honest and operational. Name owners and open gaps plainly, in the text.
- No contractions. "do not," not "don't."
- Scannable structure. Bold title carries the what; a short paragraph carries the why and how.
- One claim, one source. Sanity-check everything. Each item is one real thing under its real name; flag contradictions, do not smooth them over.
- The summary is derived from the detail. Never say anything the full version does not; re-derive it when the detail changes.
- Park references in a short trailing tag. Keep sources and tools out of the prose.
- Label every number. Never give a bare figure - say what it measures.

August 6, 2026· Quoting @mattpocockuk

Suddenly, everyone is adding ASD-STE100, Simplified Technical English, to their CLAUDE.md and thinking it will fix AI's sloppy writing. It will not.

ASD-STE100 is built for aircraft maintenance manuals. Its readers are technicians, often non-native English speakers. It forces short sentences, plain words, one instruction at a time, and a fixed dictionary of about 900 approved words, each locked to a single meaning.

But STE is voice-less by design. Every sentence is meant to read the same way, from anyone, forever.

And here is the irony. The complaint about AI writing is not that it is too colorful. It is that it is generic. Flat, hedged, saying nothing, sounding like everyone else. STE makes writing more uniform, not less. You would be fixing AI slop with a different flavor of AI slop.

Borrow what is good in it - short sentences, plain words, no jargon. But add your own writing tenets.

AI slop is a thinking problem, not a vocabulary problem. STE constrains words. It does nothing about thought.

July 10, 2026

Some field notes from building AI agents.

1. An agent silently skips the steps that produce no visible output. A step that prints a line, even one that says "nothing", reliably runs. So make every required step leave a mark, and treat a missing mark as a defect.

2. An agent confirming to you that the work happened doesn't mean it actually happened. A rendered "done" could be just another confident self-report hallucinated by the agent. The real test of completion is the file on disk, not the text on the screen.

3. How do you know an agent ran the real step? Put a value in the output that the model cannot reproduce, such as a version number or a hash. If that value is present, the step ran. If it is missing or stale, the agent hallucinated.

4. Almost every real bug I found came from shipping a small change and watching what actually reached the disk. It did not come from unit test cases where I thought about what might go wrong. The invisible failures mean you cannot reach them by thinking. Ship, then look.

5. Sometimes an agent cannot solve a problem. Not because the problem is hard, but because the agent is buried in too much context. The fix is not more context. Start a fresh sub-agent with none. It gets the same problem and a clean slate, and it often solves in minutes what the first one could not.

6. Docs decay. Atoms do not. Word documents and slides get versioned, archived, and forgotten. The knowledge was real; the format let it rot. Distill each document to its core points, and store those as small plain files - atoms - that your AI reads directly.

7. You do not need a frontier model for every agent. Use the expensive model for the supervisor that plans and reviews the work. The workers under it handle narrow, well-defined tasks, so cheaper open-source models are enough.

8. Do not keep your AI's memory in a vendor's database. Keep it in a place that you own. Then, if you switch models or lose one, you lose nothing. I move across models without losing context.

9. Do not run everything on a single model. Spread your work across several. When one goes down or degrades, the others keep running.

How are you guys managing context memory in Claude Cowork?

Approach I was using: A custom "Commit" command that wrote facts directly into memory files mid-chat and saved copies of those memory files in the working folder. Snapshots taken manually when needed or after compacting.

Approach I use now: Daily journal entries from chats. Claude classifies the conversation's durable facts and writes them to typed atomic files (feedback/, projects/, reference/, people/) plus appends the journal entry. A weekly scheduled task handles cleanup - dedup, rotate snapshots, and regenerate MEMORY.md indexes.

Each project has CLAUDE.md + TASKS.md + memory folder with journal/, feedback/, projects/, reference/, people/, snapshots/. A single global layer holds cross-project preferences.

Any better approach - without piping into a third-party "Brain"?
